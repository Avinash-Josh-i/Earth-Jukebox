Why it sounds like this

Air records. NASA's GEOS-CF model gives hourly surface concentrations of fine particles (PM2.5), ozone, carbon monoxide and nitrogen dioxide. PM2.5 is the kick drum: the dirtier the air, the faster and heavier it beats. Ozone is a bell that climbs a pentatonic scale. Carbon monoxide is a pad that gets louder and brighter. Nitrogen dioxide is a hi-hat style tick that speeds up. When PM2.5 passes the "unhealthy" range the pad's calm fifth bends towards a tense tritone and its filter sharpens, so danger is something you can hear, not only read.

Scale. By default each pollutant is mapped against a fixed health-based range, so a clean week and a polluted week sound different. The chart's dashed line is the WHO 24-hour PM2.5 guideline (15 µg/m³). "This window only" rescales to the lowest and highest values shown, which is useful for hearing the daily rhythm but hides how bad the air is.

Image records. The picture is read as a score, left to right. The average hue chooses the key. Warm images use a minor scale, cool ones a major scale. In each vertical slice the brightest, most saturated feature sets the melody pitch: higher in the picture means a higher note. The melody pans from left ear to right ear in step with the scan line. Slice brightness opens the pad's filter and sets loudness. Dark slices add a low sub tone. Sharp edges add light ticks, and detailed images play more notes. Smooth gradients play fewer, longer ones. Drag the position slider while paused to hear any slice.

Credits. Air data comes from NASA GMAO GEOS-CF. Image credits are shown at the bottom of the page for each record.

Keyboard shortcuts
  • Space or K: play or pause
  • ← →: move 5% (hold Shift for 20%)
  • ↑ ↓: volume
  • 1–4: switch an instrument on or off
  • D: describe this moment
